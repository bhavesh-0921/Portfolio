Why Companies Don't Want You To Own Anything

Runtime: ~8-10 minutes. An exploration of the quiet shift from product ownership to subscription models.

[FRAME: Close shot, a car's leather seat, dashboard lights glowing softly]

In 2022, BMW tried something so strange that people genuinely thought it had to be a joke.

If your BMW came with heated seats, the seat warmers were already there. The wiring, the heating element, all of it, sitting under the leather the day you drove the car off the lot. You paid for it. It's physically inside your property.

But to actually turn the warmth on, you'd now need to pay BMW a monthly subscription.

For a feature that was already built into something you already bought. Eighteen dollars a month, for a seat warmer that was already wired in.

[FRAME: A small lock icon overlaying a car seat]

The backlash was so loud that BMW killed the program within a year. But the fact that they tried it at all? That's the part worth sitting with.

And it gets stranger. Because heated seats aren't where this story starts.

To understand where this actually comes from, we have to go back almost a hundred years. We'll get there. First, here's something you've probably lived through yourself, without realizing what it really meant.

***
[FRAME: A bookshelf, then a Kindle screen]

A few years before the BMW story, something even odder happened with books.

Thousands of people opened their Kindles one morning and found a book they had bought, fully paid for, just gone. Not hidden. Deleted, remotely, by the company that sold it.

The book was George Orwell's "1984." A story about a government that quietly erases the past whenever it's inconvenient.

People had notes in the margins. Highlights. All of it vanished overnight, with a small refund dropped in like an apology nobody asked for.

The company called it a licensing mistake. Apologized. Moved on.

But that "mistake" answered a question most of us never thought to ask.

[FRAME: Slow zoom on a phone screen full of small recurring charges]

If a company can reach into something you bought and take it back... did you ever actually own it?

***

Two completely different products. A car. A book. Same hidden pattern.

You didn't buy the thing. You bought access to it. And access can be switched off, on someone else's schedule, not yours.

Not greed, exactly. Certainty. Sell something once, and you're hoping the customer comes back. Get them on a subscription, and you don't have to hope.

Somewhere along the way, an entire economy quietly shifted its goal. Stop selling products. Start renting relationships.

[FRAME: A jagged line graph slowly smoothing into a flat, rising line]
***
[FRAME: A dim conference room, a single hanging lightbulb]

Remember that hundred-year detour? Here it is.

The strange thing is, a hundred years ago, companies weren't trying to rent you anything.

They were trying something else. Something arguably worse.

In 1924, the world's biggest lightbulb manufacturers secretly met in Geneva. Not to talk politics. Their bulbs were lasting too long, some over two thousand hours, and a bulb that never dies is a bulb you only sell once.

So they made a pact. No bulb would last more than a thousand hours. Factories that built bulbs too well were fined.

For decades, nobody knew.

That idea, build it to fail, eventually became too risky. People notice broken products. They get angry. They write articles about secret meetings in Geneva.

So the model evolved into something far more elegant. Don't break the product.

Just never let them fully own it.

***

Software figured this out first. You used to buy a program once, in a box, and use it for a decade. Then it became something you "accessed" monthly, and the moment you stopped paying, it didn't just stop updating. It stopped working entirely.

That worked so well, it quietly spread everywhere.

[FRAME: A smart home hub sitting on a shelf, a small light blinking, then fading off]

In 2014, a tech giant bought a small company that made a sleek little device called the Revolv hub. It controlled your smart lights, locks, thermostat, all from one little glowing puck on your wall. People paid around three hundred dollars for it.

Two years later, the company shut down the servers. Not because the hubs broke. They worked fine. The company just... didn't want to run them anymore.

Every single one of those devices, sitting in people's homes, instantly became an expensive paperweight. The hardware was still there. The thing you paid for, physically, was untouched.

But the permission to use it was gone.

***

Once you see this pattern, you start noticing it everywhere.

[FRAME: An ink cartridge, half full, sitting beside a printer]

Take HP's "Instant Ink" subscription. Join it, and cheap ink shows up at your door automatically. But buried in HP's own terms of service is this: if you cancel, the cartridges already sitting in your printer, even ones still half full, are designed to stop working. Not because they're empty. Because the subscription ended.

Fitness apps run a softer version of the same trick. They don't sell workouts anymore. They sell streaks and momentum, things you'll feel guilty losing the moment you stop paying.

Even farm equipment isn't exempt. Machines costing hundreds of thousands of dollars can be remotely locked by an error code only the manufacturer is "authorized" to clear. The farmer owns the tractor. They just can't always fix it.

[FRAME: A wide shot, a single tractor sitting motionless in a field]

The object is yours. The permission never was.

***
[FRAME: A "Cancel Subscription" button, surrounded by a maze of smaller links and pop-ups]

And here's the last piece, the part that makes the whole system run.

Signing up for almost anything takes one click. Cancelling almost never does. Sometimes it's a phone call. A hold queue. Someone gently asking, "are you sure?"

That's not bad design. That's very good design, just not aimed at you.

Because the model only works if a percentage of people forget. Forget they're paying. Forget that free trial from eight months ago. Multiply a few quiet dollars by a few services, by millions of people, and you start to understand why almost everything works this way now.

***

The strange thing is, ownership didn't disappear overnight.

Nobody announced it. Nobody voted on it.

One product at a time. One subscription at a time. One login at a time, we quietly got used to the idea that paying for something and owning it were the same thing.

They aren't.

[FRAME: Slow fade to black, a single thin line of light remains, slowly extending across the screen]

And once you start noticing exactly where that line gets crossed... it's hard to stop pulling on it.

[END]
